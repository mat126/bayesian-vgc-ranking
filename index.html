Who Enters Worlds as the Favourite? A Bayesian Ranking Model for Competitive Pokémon VGC

Hierarchical Bradley-Terry models, factorisation-machine synergy, and a Set Transformer — what 17,581 tournament matches actually tell us about the Regulation M-B metagame

1 The question, and why it is harder than it looks

Every August, the Pokémon Video Game Championships crown a world champion. In 2026 the tournament runs on Pokémon Champions under Regulation Set M-B — a format with Mega Evolutions, without Terastallization, and with a metagame that has had only a few months to crystallise. The question every competitive player asks in the run-up is the same one every sports analyst asks before a major: who enters as the favourite?

For most sports this is a solved problem in outline: collect head-to-head results, fit a paired-comparison model, read off the latent strengths. Elo does it for chess; my earlier Bayesian UEFA Swiss-system simulator does it for club football. VGC breaks the standard recipe in two specific places, and this project is essentially the story of repairing both.

The first break: the unit of competition is combinatorial. A VGC “competitor” is not a fixed entity like a chess player. It is a team of six Pokémon, and each Pokémon is itself a bundle of choices: four moves from a large learnset, one ability, one held item, one nature. Two teams sharing all six species can still be meaningfully different builds. The consequence is brutal for any model that assigns a free strength parameter per competitor: in my final dataset of 17,581 matches there are 5,393 distinct teams, of which only 95 appear five or more times. A classical Bradley-Terry model with one parameter per team would have roughly one observation per parameter. Nothing identifiable survives that.

The second break: the data does not come as head-to-head outcomes. The community’s best-known statistics — usage rates from Victory Road or Pikalytics — tell you how often a Pokémon is brought, not who beats whom. Usage is a popularity contest confounded with strength; a paired-comparison likelihood needs pairings and winners with both teams observed. That data exists, but it has to be assembled, and it arrives dirty in ways worth documenting.

The rest of this post walks through the pipeline (briefly), then through three models in increasing order of expressiveness — an additive hierarchical Bayesian Bradley-Terry, the same model with a low-rank synergy term, and a fully non-linear Set Transformer — with the mathematics spelled out, and finally through what the fitted models actually say about the Regulation M-B metagame. The punchline is not the ranking itself. The punchline is what the posterior uncertainty does to the ranking, and how much of the outcome signal turns out to live somewhere other than the team sheet.

2 Building a head-to-head dataset

2.1 The source

The workable source turned out to be the public Limitless API. Limitless hosts a large share of the online VGC tournament circuit, and — crucially — requires teamlist submission. Its standings endpoint returns each player’s full structured teamlist (species, item, ability, nature, four moves per slot), and its pairings endpoint returns per-round pairings with winners. Joining the two yields exactly the object a Bradley-Terry likelihood wants:

\[ (\text{team}_A,\ \text{team}_B,\ y), \qquad y = \mathbb{1}[\text{A won}]. \]

One subtlety: a pairings row is one match, not one game, so a best-of-three enters as a single Bernoulli observation with the series winner. That is coherent with the likelihood below, though it does mean a Bo3 row carries the same nominal weight as a Bo1 row.

The final pull: 17,581 decisive matches across Regulation M-B tournaments, involving 3,878 distinct players, with complete teamlists on both sides of every match.

2.2 The cleaning problem nobody warns you about

Teamlists are submitted by players in the language of their own game copy. The raw data therefore contained 56 distinct “natures” — in a game that has exactly 25. “Modest”, “Modesta”, “Modeste” and “MODESTA” all name the same thing; “Adament”, “Adman” and “Adanant” are all attempts at “Adamant”; and a nonzero number of players typed an ability into the nature field. Moves and abilities were polluted the same way (861 raw “moves”, 294 raw “abilities”).

What rescues this from being a data-cleaning nightmare is that the vocabulary is closed and known. There are exactly 25 natures, ~300 abilities, ~900 moves, each with official names in every localisation, all published in PokéAPI’s multilingual CSV tables. Canonicalisation then becomes an almost fully automatic cascade: normalise to a slug (lowercase, strip accents and punctuation), exact-match against the multilingual table (which resolves every official language in one step), fuzzy-match the residue against the canonical English list (which resolves typos — “Timmid” has exactly one plausible neighbour), and hand-map the last dozen tokens.

One class of “unresolved” tokens was deliberately left alone: the new Champions Mega Stones. Floettite (6,598 occurrences), Raichunite Y, Staraptite, Delphoxite and 49 others do not exist in PokéAPI because the game postdates it. They are internally consistent strings, so they function perfectly well as features — and, as we will see, they matter.

3 Model 1: a hierarchical Bayesian Bradley-Terry with structured strength

3.1 The likelihood

The Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) assigns each competitor a latent strength \(s \in \mathbb{R}\) and links the win probability to the strength difference through the logistic function:

\[ P(A \text{ beats } B) \;=\; \sigma\big(s(A) - s(B)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}. \]

Each match contributes a Bernoulli term, and the full log-likelihood is a sum over matches. The difference structure has a property worth making explicit because everything downstream inherits it: it enforces antisymmetry by construction, \(P(A>B) = 1 - P(B>A)\), and any constant added to all strengths cancels. Strengths are identified only up to location — which will come back as a modelling decision shortly.

Two additions to the bare likelihood. A side intercept \(c\) absorbs any systematic player1/player2 asymmetry (in this data, player1 wins 51.3% of the time — small, but real, and better modelled than ignored). And a player skill term: writing \(\pi_1(m), \pi_2(m)\) for the pilots in match \(m\),

\[ \eta_m \;=\; c \;+\; \big(s(A_m) - s(B_m)\big) \;+\; \big(\lambda_{\pi_1(m)} - \lambda_{\pi_2(m)}\big), \qquad y_m \sim \text{Bernoulli}\big(\sigma(\eta_m)\big). \]

The player term is not decoration. Strong players choose strong teams; without \(\lambda\), the skill of good pilots leaks into the coefficients of whatever they happen to bring, and the model measures who plays rather than what is played. Foreshadowing: this term will turn out to be the single most important component of the entire model.

3.2 Decomposing strength

The move that makes the combinatorics tractable is to refuse to give teams free parameters. A team \(T\) is a set of six builds; each build \(b\) has a species \(\sigma(b)\), a move-set \(\mathcal{M}(b)\) (four moves), an ability \(a(b)\), an item \(i(b)\) and a nature \(\nu(b)\). Strength is defined compositionally:

\[ s(T) \;=\; \sum_{b \in T} \varphi(b), \qquad \varphi(b) \;=\; \alpha_{\sigma(b)} \;+\; \sum_{k \in \mathcal{M}(b)} \mu_k \;+\; \beta_{a(b)} \;+\; \gamma_{i(b)} \;+\; \delta_{\nu(b)}. \]

Every effect is a log-odds contribution: \(\alpha\) for species (the anchor), \(\mu\) for moves, \(\beta\) for abilities, \(\gamma\) for items, \(\delta\) for natures. A team never seen before still receives a coherent strength, assembled from parts that recur across thousands of matches. This is what converts 5,393 unidentifiable team parameters into roughly a thousand well-shared feature parameters (219 species + 420 moves + 162 items + 168 abilities + 23 natures after frequency thresholding).

There is a computational identity hiding here that makes implementation almost embarrassingly simple. Because \(s(T)\) is linear in feature counts, collect the counts into a vector \(x_T\) (how many times each species, move, item… appears in the team — note moves genuinely count above one: a team running Protect on four slots has a Protect count of 4). Then

\[ s(A) - s(B) \;=\; \theta^\top (x_A - x_B), \]

with \(\theta\) stacking all the coefficient blocks. The additive Bradley-Terry with structured strength is a logistic regression on the difference of count vectors. The design matrix of the whole project is that difference, one row per match.

3.3 The hierarchy: why rare items do not wreck the model

Rare levels are everywhere in this data — items brought a handful of times, moves on one niche set. Maximum likelihood would assign them wild coefficients. The Bayesian answer is partial pooling through a scale hierarchy: each block gets its own dispersion parameter,

\[ \alpha_\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau_\alpha^2), \quad \mu_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau_\mu^2), \quad \ldots \quad \lambda_\pi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau_\lambda^2), \]

with half-normal hyperpriors on every \(\tau\). A rarely-seen item is shrunk toward zero in proportion to how weakly the data supports it; a frequent one is free to take whatever value the matches indicate. No arbitrary cutoff decides this — the \(\tau\)’s, estimated from the data, do.

The \(\tau\)’s also serve a second role that ends up carrying the headline result: they are a variance decomposition. \(\tau_{\text{player}}\) versus \(\tau_{\text{species}}\) answers, directly, “how much of the outcome variation is pilot, and how much is team sheet?”

Two technical points that cost real debugging time and deserve to be written down.

Identifiability. Every team has exactly six species, so adding a constant \(\kappa\) to all \(\alpha\)’s adds \(6\kappa\) to every strength and cancels in every difference. Left alone, the chains drift along this flat direction. The fix is a sum-to-zero constraint per block (PyMC’s ZeroSumNormal), after which every coefficient reads as “relative to its block average”.

The funnel. ZeroSumNormal is non-centred with respect to the sum-to-zero constraint — but not with respect to the hierarchical scale. Writing ZeroSumNormal(sigma=tau) keeps the centred parameterisation in \(\tau\), and with 1,657 player effects hanging off a single scale, that is a textbook funnel (Betancourt & Girolami, 2015): the sampler’s step size collapses (I watched chains freeze at \(\epsilon = 0.001\) while their siblings adapted to \(0.1\)). The cure is the explicit non-centred form,

\[ \tilde\lambda_\pi \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \ \text{(sum-to-zero)}, \qquad \lambda_\pi = \tau_\lambda \, \tilde\lambda_\pi, \]

which decouples the geometry of the raw variables from the scale. After this change, step sizes doubled and gradient evaluations per draw dropped from 255–1023 to a uniform 31.

Conditioning. One more practical surgery: of the 3,878 players, most appear in a handful of matches. A player with two matches has an essentially unidentified \(\lambda\) — a flat direction that forces NUTS into maximal-depth trajectories. Players below eight matches were therefore pooled to a shared zero effect (their matches remain in the likelihood and keep informing the team-level effects; only the individual skill parameter is dropped). This kept 1,657 individually-modelled players and made the difference between a sampler that saturated its tree depth and one that cruised.

3.4 Inference

Two regimes were used deliberately. ADVI (mean-field variational inference) for iteration: minutes per fit, but it approximates the posterior with a factorised Gaussian and is known to underestimate uncertainty. NUTS for the final estimates — run through the numpyro backend on a Kaggle GPU, because the dominant cost is dense matrix–vector products (\(17{,}581 \times \sim 1{,}000\) per block per gradient), which is exactly the workload GPUs eat. The GPU run converged cleanly: \(\hat R \le 1.02\) everywhere, bulk effective sample sizes in the hundreds to thousands, zero divergences.

The ADVI-versus-NUTS comparison is itself a result worth reporting: the NUTS posterior scales came out at roughly half the ADVI values across every block, and intervals widened substantially. Every “finding” that looked crisp under ADVI had to be re-litigated under the real posterior — and, as the results section shows, most species-level findings did not survive. If you take one methodological message from this post: do not publish variational intervals for hierarchical models without checking them against MCMC.

4 Model 2: low-rank synergy — a factorisation machine inside the likelihood

The additive model has a visible blind spot: it prices every Pokémon independently of its teammates. VGC players will tell you this is wrong — the game is built around cores, weather engines, Trick Room modes. The value of a redirection support depends on what it redirects for.

The naive fix — a free interaction \(\omega_{ss'}\) for every species pair — needs \(\binom{219}{2} \approx 24{,}000\) parameters, most for pairs never observed together. The scalable fix is low-rank: give each species a latent vector \(v_s \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (I used \(d=4\)) and let pairwise synergy be an inner product. The team-level synergy term is

\[ \psi(T) \;=\; \sum_{\{s,s'\} \subseteq T,\ s \ne s'} \langle v_s, v_{s'} \rangle , \]

which is precisely the second-order term of a factorisation machine (Rendle, 2010). Two properties make it attractive here. First, the closed form

\[ \psi(T) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\Big( \big\lVert \textstyle\sum_{s \in T} v_s \big\rVert^2 \;-\; \sum_{s \in T} \lVert v_s \rVert^2 \Big) \]

computes it in \(O(kd)\) instead of \(O(k^2 d)\) — the squared sum contains all cross terms plus the diagonal, and subtracting the norms removes exactly the diagonal. Second, that same subtraction guarantees a species generates no self-synergy: solo value stays entirely in \(\alpha_s\), and \(\psi\) captures only what emerges from the pairing. The decomposition between main effect and interaction is clean by construction.

One implementation consequence: \(\psi\) is quadratic in the species counts, so \(\psi(A) - \psi(B)\) does not reduce to a function of \(x_A - x_B\). The synergy model needs per-side count matrices, not just their difference — the one place where the tidy “logistic regression on differences” picture breaks.

The latent vectors get a deliberately tight prior (\(\tau_{\text{syn}} \sim \text{HalfNormal}(0.2)\), non-centred): the quadratic term should only claim variance the additive part leaves on the table, and a loose prior here destabilised ADVI badly enough to produce NaN cascades before it was tightened.

An interpretive note before reading any synergy output: inner products are invariant to rotations of the latent space, so individual coordinates of \(v_s\) mean nothing. The identified quantities are the pairwise products \(\langle v_s, v_{s'} \rangle\) and the total \(\psi(T)\) — read those, never the axes.

5 Model 3: beyond linearity — a Siamese Set Transformer

The factorisation machine is still a degree-2 polynomial in the features. If the metagame contains genuinely higher-order structure — a Pokémon whose value depends on the joint presence of two specific partners, mode-based teams whose strength is not any sum of pairs — neither Bayesian model can represent it. The third model asks the question directly: does letting a neural network learn the strength function buy predictive accuracy?

The architecture is not a generic MLP, and the reasons why are the interesting part. Two structural facts about the problem dictate almost everything:

Antisymmetry must survive. This is achieved with a Siamese design: one tower \(f_\theta\) (shared weights) scores each team to a scalar, and the logit is the difference,

\[ \eta \;=\; f_\theta(A) - f_\theta(B) + c , \]

so \(P(A>B) = 1 - P(B>A)\) holds by construction, exactly as in Bradley-Terry. A network that concatenated both teams and classified would have to learn antisymmetry from data, and would learn it imperfectly.

Double permutation invariance must survive. The four moves of a Pokémon are an unordered set; the six Pokémon of a team are an unordered set. Flattening a team into a fixed-position vector would force the network to re-learn “Incineroar” separately for every slot — a catastrophic waste of 17k matches. The tower therefore reads, bottom to top:

  1. Embeddings for each categorical feature (species, move, ability, item, nature; \(d=24\)), giving every symbol one shared learned vector regardless of position. This is the neural analogue of the Bayesian coefficients — parameter sharing as statistical strength.
  2. Mean-pooling over the four move embeddings — a symmetric function, so move order is irrelevant by construction, not by training.
  3. A per-Pokémon MLP (\(5d \to h \to h\), \(h=64\), shared across the six slots) fusing the feature bundle non-linearly — an item’s value may depend on the ability next to it.
  4. Self-attention across the six Pokémon (a small Transformer encoder, no positional encoding). This is where synergy lives: each Pokémon’s representation is updated as an attention-weighted combination of its teammates, \(\text{Attn}(\Phi) = \text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\,V\), so an Incineroar next to a Trick Room setter is literally represented differently from an Incineroar on a hyper-offence team. Omitting positional encodings keeps the map permutation-equivariant.
  5. Mean-pooling over the six (equivariant map followed by symmetric pool ⇒ invariant function — the Deep Sets / Set Transformer recipe (Zaheer et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2019)), then a scalar head.

Training: binary cross-entropy, Adam with weight decay \(10^{-4}\) (which is, read through a Bayesian lens, a Gaussian prior on the weights — the network’s version of shrinkage), dropout, early stopping on validation log-loss, and free data augmentation by swapping A/B with flipped labels, which reinforces antisymmetry. The final model has 106,738 parameters — deliberately small, because with 17k matches and sparse features, capacity is the enemy. Uncertainty at inference time comes from MC-dropout: dropout stays active and each team is scored repeatedly, the spread standing in for a predictive interval.

6 Results

Everything below is from the NUTS posteriors (4 chains × 800 draws, \(\hat R \le 1.02\)) unless labelled otherwise. Read every table by interval, not by mean: an effect whose 90% interval crosses zero is not established by this data.

6.1 The headline: the pilot dwarfs the team sheet

The posterior block scales, additive model:

Block \(\tau\) (posterior mean) interpretation
player 0.550 dominant, and precisely estimated (0.50–0.60)
ability 0.107
item 0.102
species 0.096
moves 0.077
nature 0.058 smallest

Player skill carries roughly five times the dispersion of any team feature. The three leading team blocks — ability, item, species — have almost completely overlapping intervals and should be read as statistically indistinguishable from each other; only their collective gap to the player term is unambiguous.

Put plainly: in online Regulation M-B tournaments, who is playing predicts outcomes far better than what they are playing. Team choice matters — the team blocks are not zero — but it is a second-order effect next to the pilot.

One honest caveat before this becomes a slogan. Player skill and team strength separate only through the player × team crossing in the data. A pilot who always brings the same list makes the two partially collinear, and some team strength can hide inside \(\lambda\). The magnitude of the gap makes the qualitative conclusion robust; the exact ratio should not be quoted as a causal decomposition.

6.2 No single Pokémon is a statistically established “best pick”

This is the result that changed most between ADVI and NUTS, and the one I find most interesting. Under the full posterior, zero of 219 species have an effect whose interval clears zero. The top of the table:

Species mean 5%–95%
Kangaskhan 0.107 −0.026 – 0.266
Archaludon 0.102 −0.024 – 0.248
Eternal Flower Floette 0.100 −0.019 – 0.232
Charizard 0.096 −0.017 – 0.218
Froslass 0.094 −0.032 – 0.238

Under ADVI’s too-narrow intervals, Charizard and Floette had looked like solid positive effects. The real posterior says: plausible, not established. Given the player result above, this is coherent rather than disappointing — once pilot skill absorbs its share of the variance, the marginal effect of one species slot out of six is simply small relative to what 17k noisy Bernoulli outcomes can pin down. The data supports a metagame reading in which no single species is an auto-win button; value lives in whole configurations and in the hands holding them.

6.3 Where the signal is: moves, items, natures

Interestingly, identifiability improves as we move from “which Pokémon” to “which choices”. Twelve moves clear zero, and their identity sketches a coherent picture of what wins in M-B — control, positioning and utility over raw damage:

Move mean 5%–95%
Encore 0.121 0.053 – 0.190
Weather Ball 0.115 0.043 – 0.189
Quick Guard 0.112 0.013 – 0.220
Swords Dance 0.107 0.031 – 0.185
Stomping Tantrum 0.100 0.033 – 0.167
Thunderbolt 0.082 0.017 – 0.150

(Also robust: Low Kick, Protect, Life Dew, Liquidation, Ice Beam, Thunder.) Encore leading the format is a very “doubles” result: tempo denial beats power. Weather Ball’s position points at active weather engines.

Seven items clear zero, led by Venusaurite (0.143, 0.018–0.282) — Mega Venusaur’s stone, and the single largest robust coefficient among all features — with Light Clay (screens support) and Passho Berry alongside. The Mega Stones’ presence at the top of the item table confirms the format-defining role of Mega Evolution; Floettite actually has the highest posterior mean (0.143) but its interval grazes zero (−0.004), a reminder that this format’s most hyped legendary is popular and good but not unambiguously so once uncertainty is honest. No Item also comes out robustly non-zero — running itemless is a real strategic signal, not a data hole.

Among natures, three are robust — Calm (0.091), Modest (0.064), Bold (0.055) — all special-side or defensive, consistent with reading nature as a proxy for the build archetype the EV spread (deliberately excluded from the model) would have revealed.

Two abilities clear zero: Unnerve and Swift Swim. The ability block suffers from an extra handicap — roughly 8,500 slots have the ability missing in the source data, handled by partial pooling rather than row deletion, but inevitably widening those posteriors.

2 Blastoise / Delphox / Sneasler / Incineroar / Sinistcha / Kingambit 5 1.90 1.55 – 2.26
3 Delphox / Eternal Flower Floette / Sinistcha / Kingambit / Sneasler / Incineroar 7 1.82 1.48 – 2.16

The top of the table is essentially one archetype in variations: a Sneasler / Kingambit / Incineroar / Sinistcha core flexing its last two slots (Blastoise + Delphox, or Floette + Delphox). Rows 1 and 2 share their entire six-species line-up and differ only in sets, and the model prices that set difference at ~0.15 log-odds — a nice illustration of why modelling below the species level was worth the trouble. The synergy model reproduces this ranking almost unchanged (same top three, slightly higher strengths), which is itself informative: pairwise synergy refines, it does not overturn.

A head-to-head worth quoting for calibration: the model gives team 1 a 54.7% [47.6%, 62.2%] probability against team 2 at equal pilot skill. Even the top team’s edge over its nearest rival brushes a coin flip. Nobody should read this table as a tier list with cliffs in it.

6.5 Synergy: real, small, and concentrated

Out of 1,936 species pairs observed together at least five times, exactly four have robustly positive synergy:

Pair n (co-occurrences) \(\langle v_s, v_{s'} \rangle\) 5%–95%
Archaludon + Grimmsnarl 495 0.095 0.003 – 0.206
Kingambit + Sneasler 567 0.091 0.019 – 0.172
Kingambit + Sinistcha 388 0.089 0.019 – 0.170
E. F. Floette + Kingambit 571 0.080 0.007 – 0.162

The list reads like a who’s-who of known cores: Archaludon under Grimmsnarl screens (Light Clay’s robust item effect is the same engine seen from another block), and Kingambit appearing in three of the four pairs — the model has learned, from win/loss data alone, that Kingambit is the glue of the format’s dominant archetype. No negative pair reaches robustness, though Kingambit’s anti-synergies with Farigiraf, Archaludon and Grimmsnarl hover near the boundary — plausibly the model noticing role redundancy.

The fact that the robust synergy pairs sit exactly inside the top teams is the kind of internal consistency you hope for but should still check: the FM term and the team ranking were estimated jointly, but nothing forced the interpretable pairs to align with the empirically successful rosters.

6.6 Did non-linearity help? No — and that is a result

The comparison that motivated the whole third model:

Model held-out log-loss held-out accuracy
Coin flip 0.6931 0.500
Bayesian additive (ADVI refit) 0.6618 0.614
Bayesian additive (NUTS refit) 0.6584 0.601
Set Transformer 0.6692 ~0.61

The network does not beat the linear-in-features Bayesian model. The rigorous comparison — a full NUTS refit on an 85/15 training split, scored on the 2,638 held-out matches — puts the Bayesian model at 0.6584, the best number of the three, with the Set Transformer at 0.6692. Proper Hamiltonian sampling therefore does not only produce honest intervals; it also predicts slightly better than its variational approximation. (One honesty note: the neural split is equal in size but not identical row-for-row to the Bayesian one — different RNGs — so the neural gap should be read as “clearly not better” rather than as a defeat by exactly that margin.)

I want to resist the temptation to spin this. The Set Transformer was the right architecture for the hypothesis — attention over the six-slot set is precisely how you would let higher-order synergy express itself — and it was trained with the right regularisation for the data scale. It found nothing the additive model plus degree-2 synergy had missed. The conclusion I actually draw is a statement about the game, conditional on this data: at 17k matches, whatever higher-order interaction structure exists in the M-B metagame is not strong enough to be learnable past the noise floor set by unobserved in-battle decisions and the pilot effect. The regularised Bayesian model is not a baseline the deep model failed to beat; it is the right-sized model for the problem.

The neural model’s team ranking is worth a glance for what it disagrees about: its top team (Incineroar / Gardevoir / Talonflame / Tsareena / Maushold / Basculegion) is nowhere near the Bayesian podium. With near-identical held-out loss, disagreements of this kind are mostly a display of estimation variance between two models fitting the same weak signal — a caution against over-reading any single model’s team list.

6.7 How predictable is a VGC match, anyway?

It is worth dwelling on the absolute numbers. The best model reaches log-loss 0.658 against a 0.693 coin flip and roughly 60% accuracy. That is a genuine edge — over thousands of matches it is decisive information — but it means the single match remains close to a fair coin even when you know both full teamlists and the pilots’ historical strength. The unmodelled residue is the game itself: open team sheet reading, leads, in-battle decisions, and honest-to-goodness RNG. Any service claiming to predict individual VGC matches with high confidence is selling something the data says does not exist.

7 What this means for Worlds — and why it is already going stale

Pulling the threads together for the question we started with:

  • The archetype entering as the favourite is the Sneasler / Kingambit / Incineroar / Sinistcha core with flexible fifth and sixth slots — top of both Bayesian rankings, glued together by three of the four robustly positive synergy pairs in the entire format.
  • The robust individual signals are choices, not species: Encore, Weather Ball, Quick Guard, Swords Dance among moves; Venusaurite, Light Clay, Passho Berry among items; calm, special-leaning natures. No single Pokémon is a statistically established best pick.
  • The biggest single predictor of who wins is who is playing. Team choice is worth perhaps a fifth of the pilot, by posterior dispersion. Practice beats teambuilding — or more precisely, teambuilding differences among competent players are worth less than skill differences among them.

And the necessary disclaimer, stated as a modelling assumption rather than a shrug: this entire analysis is a snapshot under stationarity. The model assumes the win-generating process is constant within the Regulation M-B window, and the metagame demonstrably is not — it adapts weekly, and it will adapt to whatever is currently winning, Sneasler cores included. There is also a population gap: this is online Limitless data, and the Worlds field (invite-only, in-person, best-of-three throughout) is a different distribution. The pipeline is built to be re-run — new tournaments in, canonicalisation, refit — and the honest way to use this model in August is on data pulled in August. Planned extensions, roughly in order: exponential time-decay of match weights (a power-posterior compromise), a row-for-row shared validation split across all three models (the NUTS and neural numbers above use equal-sized but independently drawn splits), extending the factorisation to move-and-item vectors, and a low-rank antisymmetric matchup term for the rock-paper-scissors structure a single strength scale cannot express.

8 Reproducibility

Everything — collection client, canonicaliser, design matrices, all three models, and the analysis notebooks — is at github.com/mat126/bayesian-ranking-vgc. The stack: PyMC with the numpyro/JAX backend on GPU for sampling (nutpie on CPU for iteration), PyTorch for the Set Transformer, PokéAPI’s multilingual tables plus rapidfuzz for cleaning. Posteriors are ~130–175 MB NetCDF files, regenerable from the pipeline; the run configuration travels inside a tagged artifacts JSON precisely so that no two posteriors can be compared without knowing they were estimated under the same thresholds.
